# Kostas's Written Voice — for the portfolio site

> **Purpose:** characterize how Kostas writes (vs. how he speaks) well enough that any blog post, widget caption, page subtitle, alt text, or piece of UI copy can be checked against this profile and rewritten if it doesn't match.
>
> Adapted from `~/Insync/Gdrive/Projects/jobs/communication-profile/synthesis/spoken-voice.md`. The spoken profile captures interview-call delivery (long compound sentences, fillers, restarts, modal-conditional asks). Most of that doesn't translate to written copy — written copy gets read silently, often skimmed, and editors can iterate. So written voice is **tighter than spoken voice but built from the same principles**: hedged, specific, modest, anti-corporate, slightly Greek-flavored.

---

## 0. The fingerprint (one-page summary)

**Sentence shape:** mixed. One short punchy sentence. Then one longer with a *"so / but / and"* clause. The pattern is rhythm, not uniformity. Avoid 60-word run-on sentences (that's spoken voice, not written).

**Person:** first person throughout. *I / my* freely. Never strips the subject for "professional polish." A blog post is Kostas talking to a reader, not a CV bullet.

**Hedges:** *"I think"*, *"I'd say"*, *"to be honest"*, *"in my case"*, *"from my experience"* are present but used about half as often as in speech. One per paragraph at most. They earn their keep on judgment claims, not on facts.

**Modesty markers (this is the strongest signal):**
- *"a bit of"* — *"a bit of medical AI too"*
- *"so far"* — *"10 papers and 102+ citations so far"*
- *"a few"* — *"a few papers along the way"*, *"a few detours in between"*
- *"mostly"* — *"mostly self-supervised learning"*
- *"and counting"* — to soften a number without flexing it

These are everywhere in his existing site copy. They're the single most identifying feature of his written voice.

**Tone:** soft confidence. Direct on substance, never pushy. Shares what he's actually done without inflating it. The opposite of LinkedIn thought-leader voice.

**No emojis in copy** (unless he explicitly asks). His existing widgets use them deliberately and rarely.

**No em-dashes or en-dashes as separators.** Site rule: *use `:` instead*. Em-dashes inside quoted academic abstracts are fine; in his own copy they're not.

**Greek-influence:** light traces of Greek-as-L1 phrasing are OK in personal writing (blog posts in his voice). Avoid them in widget UI copy and page subtitles where they read as typos.

## 1. Sentence patterns

### 1.1 Mixed rhythm

> "Mostly self-supervised learning, LLMs, computer vision, and remote sensing. A bit of medical AI too."
>
> "10 papers across NeurIPS, WACV, IGARSS, CHASE, and ECCV: 102+ citations and counting. The work centers on self-supervised learning, computer vision, and remote sensing."

Two sentences, different lengths. That's the default. A monolithic single 60-word sentence is too spoken; three identical 8-word sentences are too marketing-deck.

### 1.2 The colon pivot

He uses `:` as a separator a lot. Replaces em-dashes (banned) and "comma + reason" constructions:

> "Topics I've published in: self-supervised learning, LLMs, computer vision, remote sensing, and a bit of medical AI."
>
> "10 papers across NeurIPS, WACV, IGARSS, CHASE, and ECCV: 102+ citations and counting."

Pattern: *[lead]: [list / detail / consequence]*. Reads cleaner than `—` or `, with`.

### 1.3 Concrete after general

Same as spoken voice: pair a claim with an example or specific. A subtitle that just says *"I work on self-supervised learning"* is too generic; *"Mostly self-supervised learning, LLMs, computer vision, and remote sensing. A bit of medical AI too"* names the actual venues of the work.

### 1.4 First-person honesty

Site copy should sound like the author, not the brand:

| Brand voice (avoid) | Kostas voice |
|---|---|
| "Cutting-edge research at the intersection of ML and remote sensing." | "I work on self-supervised learning for remote sensing, mostly." |
| "Subscribe to never miss an update." | "Pick the streams you want — posts, papers, code." |
| "Let's connect!" | "Drop a line if you want to chat about ML or research." |

## 2. Voice principles (carry over from spoken voice)

The 6 principles from the spoken-voice doc apply to writing. Quick recap:

### A1. No corporate / MBA jargon

Banned: *leverage, drive, spearhead, scale (verb), optimize (business sense), runway, traction, alignment (business), stakeholders, ownership (corporate), at-bats, KPIs, cadence (business), bandwidth (for time), motion, deliverables, north star, moving the needle, unlocking value*.

Rule: describe the actual concrete thing. *Leverage* → *use*. *Cadence* → *how often*.

### A2. No spatial metaphors for abstract concepts

Banned: *where X sits, watching for, the table, on my radar, in my wheelhouse, where this lands, how it stacks up, pull on this thread, unpack, flesh out, bake in, step back (mental)*.

Replacements: *which is X*, *what's most important*, *develop*, *include from the start*, *think more broadly*.

### A3. No hollow generic descriptors

Banned: *productive, good fit, great opportunity, exciting role, strong opener, valuable, real value, good problem, interesting challenge (as filler)*.

If something's positive, say *what specifically* is positive. *"Interesting"* alone is fine if it's actually interesting. *"Very interesting"* is in his vocabulary.

### A3b. Words that aren't in his vocabulary

Words native English speakers reach for that Kostas does not use. Banned everywhere on the site:

- *folks* — colloquial American. Use *people*, *researchers*, *authors*, or just rephrase.
- *bylines* — journalist/American. Replace with *papers*, *publications*, *co-authored work*.
- *peeps* / *y'all* / *gang* / *crew* — same family.
- *stint* — American journalistic filler for "brief role/stay." Replace with *role*, *position*, plain temporal phrasing (*"I spent a year at..."*, *"the summer at..."*), or just describe what the work was. Same family: *gig*, *side gig*, *side hustle*, *chops*. These read as Forbes-profile or LinkedIn copy, not as someone actually telling their story.

Test: would Kostas, writing a polite email in English, type this word? If you can't picture it, it's not in his vocabulary.

The deeper pattern: avoid the **biographical-compression vocabulary** that writers use to summarize someone else's career in a sentence. Kostas is writing about himself. He has time to say *"in summer 2025 I interned at Amazon"* instead of compressing into *"a stint at Amazon."*

### A4. No native-speaker American idioms

Banned: *click, land (as fit), comes/came down to, throwing X at Y, in the same universe, in my wheelhouse, on my radar, deep dive, circle back, touch base, low-hanging fruit, ballpark, step forward (career), wake up engaged, things going for, at the end of the day, bottom line, for what it's worth, to be fair*.

**Also banned in CTAs and contact prompts** (added 2026-04-26 after a copy review):
- *drop a line* / *drop me a line* — letter-writing slang. Use *send me a message*, *email me*, *get in touch*, *write to me*, *reach me at...*
- *shoot me an email* — same family. Use *email me*.
- *hit me up* — same family. Use *get in touch*.
- *ping me* / *DM me* (when not technical) — Slack/Twitter slang. Use *message me* or *email me*.
- *let's chat* / *let's catch up* / *let's connect* — LinkedIn-speak. Use *happy to chat about...*, *get in touch about...*, *open to talking about...*

These are bar-talk for SF natives, performative for Kostas. The test: would a Greek PhD writing a polite email use this phrase? If no, find a more neutral alternative.

### A5 (asking questions) and A6 (subject-stripping in acknowledgments) are mostly spoken-voice rules; they apply less to written copy. Subject-stripping IS allowed in widget headings and labels — *"Subscribe via RSS"* is fine, *"Frequent co-authors"* is fine. Bare imperatives in CTAs are fine — *"Drop a line"*, *"Subscribe"*, *"Read more"* — they're not the same as the conversational *"Walk me through"* that A5 bans.

## 3. Vocabulary

### 3.1 Words he uses (carry over from spoken, lighter touch in writing)

**Hedges:** *I think, I'd say, to be honest, in my case, from my experience, mostly, so far*. Use sparingly in writing — once per paragraph max.

**Modesty markers (use freely):**
- *a bit of*, *a few*, *some*, *along the way*, *and counting*, *mostly*, *so far*, *a couple*

**Intensifiers:** *very, really, much, much more*. Less doubled in writing than in speech (spoken: *"very very interesting"*; written: *"very interesting"*).

**Connectors:** *so, but, and, because*. Use them — site copy has commas and periods, not bullet-pointed staccato.

**Connotation-loaded:**
- *interesting* / *very interesting* — high praise. He uses this for things that genuinely engage him intellectually.
- *fascinating* — stronger than interesting.
- *enjoyed* / *really enjoyed* — for past positive experiences (blog post about a project, a write-up about a talk).
- *flexible* — when describing approach.

### 3.2 Words and phrases NEVER in his copy

Already covered in §2 (the principles). Specific written-copy banned list:

- *Em-dashes / en-dashes as separators* — site rule: use `:` (rule from CLAUDE.md). En-dashes inside quoted academic abstracts (e.g., `2.5–2.9 mIoU`) are exempt.
- *Slop adjectives:* delve, pivotal, testament, vibrant, underscore, emphasize, foster, showcase, tapestry, interplay, intricate, enduring, crucial (as filler), Additionally (as paragraph opener)
- *Promotional language:* boasts, nestled, stunning, breathtaking, groundbreaking, must-read, renowned, rich (figurative)
- *AI-buzzword tells:* leverage → use; synergistic → cooperative; paradigm shift → major change; seamless / intuitive / powerful (pick the specific quality)
- *Filler bloat:* in order to → to; has the ability to → can; at this point in time → now; it is important to note that → delete
- *Sycophantic artifacts:* "I hope this helps!", "Certainly!", "Great question!", "Let me know if..."
- *Generic positive conclusions:* "exciting times ahead", "a step in the right direction", "the future is bright" — replace with one concrete fact or next step

## 4. Tone for different contexts on the site

### 4.1 Page titles (h1)

Casual, first-person, modest. Examples:

- "Things I've published" (publications)
- "Hello world!" (blog post)
- "How I got here" (blog post)
- "I like sharing my experiments and knowledge with others" (homepage section)
- "A quick snapshot of what I do" (homepage section)
- "My site is a playful sandbox. Explore, experiment, and say hello"

Avoid: *"Research Excellence"*, *"My Publications"*, *"Latest Articles"*, *"About Me"* (formal noun-phrase headers).

### 4.2 Subtitles / descriptions (1-2 sentences under an h1)

Concrete, modesty-marked, often with a colon pivot:

- "Mostly self-supervised learning, LLMs, computer vision, and remote sensing. A bit of medical AI too."
- "The long version of my background, from Greece to a PhD in Tennessee, with a few detours in between."
- "A fresh site, a fond farewell to the old one, and a small tease of what's coming next."

### 4.3 Widget descriptions (one short sentence)

These need to be tight (15-20 words). Specific, never marketing-y:

| Widget | Bad | Good (his voice) |
|---|---|---|
| RSS | "Subscribe to our newsletter for cutting-edge insights." | "One feed for everything I publish. Toggle what you want to follow." |
| Co-authors | "Distinguished researchers I have collaborated with." | "People I've published with." |
| Contact | "Get in touch to discuss collaboration opportunities." | "Drop me a message about anything." |

### 4.4 CTAs (buttons / links)

Short, imperative (this is one of the few spots imperatives are fine):

- "Drop a line"
- "Subscribe"
- "Read more"
- "Copy link"
- "Show full abstract" / "Hide full abstract"

NOT: *"Click here"*, *"Learn more"*, *"Get started"*, *"Discover"*.

### 4.5 Blog posts (long-form)

Full personality. Have opinions. React to facts. Vary rhythm — short sentences. Then longer ones. Acknowledge complexity. First-person openly. Some mess (tangents, half-formed thoughts) is fine.

The full blog-writing pipeline (research-first structure, anti-slop edit pass, etc.) lives in `CLAUDE.md` under "Writing Blog Posts" and `.claude/skills/blog-post`. The voice principles in this doc apply to that pipeline as well.

### 4.6 Image alt text

Plain descriptive. *"Photo of Kostas presenting at NeurIPS 2023"* not *"A confident young researcher engaging with the academic community"*.

## 5. Greek-influence rules in writing

In the spoken-voice doc, Greek-as-L1 traces (article overuse, mild tense slippage, *"like"* as approximation, stacked conditionals) are explicitly preserved. In writing, the rule is softer:

| Context | Rule |
|---|---|
| Blog posts (personal, first-person) | Light traces OK. Don't over-correct. *"Even though I enjoy research, it has been a lot of other external projects"* sounds like him in writing too. |
| Widget UI copy / subtitles | Clean it up if it would read as a typo. A subtitle is read silently and quickly; ambiguity hurts more than personality helps. |
| Image alt text / aria labels / metadata | Standard English. These are read by screen readers and indexers. |

Specifically:
- **Article slips** (extra/missing *the*/*a*) — fix in UI copy, allow in blog posts.
- **Light tense slippage** — fix everywhere except blog post personal narration.
- ***"Like"* as approximation marker** — fine in casual blog copy (*"like five hours overlap"*); avoid in widgets.
- **Stacking conditionals** (*"if because"*) — read this as a typo in writing; rewrite always.
- **Plural/singular agreement on collective nouns** — fix everywhere.

## 6. Quick test for any written line

Before shipping copy, run this 6-question check:

1. **Does it have a hedge or a modesty marker?** (For a non-trivial claim. *"The work centers on…"* is fine without; *"My research is groundbreaking"* needs to die.)
2. **Are there any banned business / MBA / metaphor / idiom words?** (See §2.)
3. **Are there em-dashes/en-dashes as separators?** (Replace with `:` or restructure.)
4. **Does it use first person where appropriate?** (Site copy that talks about Kostas should say *I*, not *we* or third person.)
5. **Could a confident American startup founder say this?** (If yes, rewrite to be more hedged and less inflated.)
6. **Is there a concrete specific?** (Not just *"interesting research"* — name what.)

If any check fails, rewrite.
