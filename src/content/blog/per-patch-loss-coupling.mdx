## Start with the picture

ExPLoRe is a method we published at ECCV 2026, with Maofeng Tang and my advisor Hairong Qi. It targets a specific problem in masked image modeling: when you pretrain with several losses at once, existing methods weight them with one global number per loss. ExPLoRe lets the model learn a separate weight for every image patch instead, by reusing the routing weights that a Soft-MoE already computes. The [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.31201) and [code](https://github.com/aicip/ExPLoRe) are written for reviewers. This post is the version I'd tell a colleague.

![Dispatch weights from a 2-expert ExPLoRe model overlaid on input images of dogs and birds. Expert 1 concentrates weight on foreground (faces, bodies); Expert 2 spreads weight to background and context.](/blog/per-patch-loss-coupling/expert-dispatch.png)

What you are looking at is a 2-expert router that nobody told where the foreground was. The model trained from scratch on ImageNet patches with one signal: a frozen CLIP teacher's per-patch features. The router decides, per patch and per loss, who pays attention to what. After 300 epochs it learned that one expert wants to weight the loss heavily on the animal, and the other wants to weight it on the background. I did not write that down anywhere. There is no foreground-vs-background supervision in the loss. It just falls out of letting loss gradients update the router.

## Why global scalars fall short

The paper before this, MEDiC, combined three masked-image-modeling signals into one student encoder: CLIP token distillation, CLS alignment, and pixel reconstruction. The framework worked. The numbers were competitive. But the way it weighted the three losses was a hack and I always knew it. One scalar per loss. Hand-tuned on a held-out subset of ImageNet. Same scalars for every image, every patch, every epoch.

The thing that bothered me about that, which I could not at first articulate, is that an image is not uniform. A patch full of texture, like grass or fur, has very different reconstruction error than a patch on a face. A patch with strong semantic content, the eye of a bird for example, has very different distillation signal than a patch of sky. If you weight all three losses with one scalar per loss, you are pretending the optimal blend is the same everywhere on the image. It is not.

I spent most of a year trying to make the global scalars work harder. I tried attention-based masking, which biases which patches the model has to reconstruct. I tried per-objective scheduling so that the three losses come in at different epochs. I tried GradNorm to auto-balance gradient magnitudes across objectives. I hand-tuned around fifty configurations and spent close to a year refreshing a Weights & Biases tab every few hours to see if the k-NN line had moved. It mostly had not. (I wrote about that stretch in [a separate post on the PhD](/blog/what-the-phd-gave-me).)

The thing that actually killed the global-scalar hope, though, was running [GradNorm](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.02257) carefully. GradNorm is the principled answer to the question "how do I weight competing objectives in multi-task learning". It normalizes gradient magnitudes across tasks and adapts per step. On our Token+CLS setup it landed at 72.8% k-NN, which is worse than the static-weight baseline at 74.2%. On Token+Pixel it collapsed entirely to 32.5%. Not a regression, a collapse. That number is in the paper because I wanted reviewers to see what we saw. A principled global method, run with extensive hyperparameter search, underperforming its own static baseline. The lesson I took from that is that the failure was not about which scalar value to pick. It was about scalars being the wrong shape.

## Soft-MoE dispatch weights, and the trick

I had been reading Soft-MoE ([Puigcerver et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.00951)) for unrelated reasons. Soft-MoE replaces some MLP layers in a transformer with a small set of expert networks, and it routes each patch to each expert with a soft, fully differentiable matrix. Two matrices, actually. The shared routing logits get softmax-normalized along two different axes:

- The dispatch matrix `D`, normalized over patches per expert. For each expert, the dispatch weights across patches sum to one. This is "which patches does this expert want to hear from".
- The combine matrix `C`, normalized over experts per patch. For each patch, the combine weights across experts sum to one. This is "which experts get to write to this patch's output".

The full architecture sits inside the student encoder at alternating ViT blocks {1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11}. Six MoE blocks, six vanilla blocks, about 1.5× the compute of a vanilla ViT-Base. The frozen CLIP teacher hands down per-patch features, the student's MoE blocks process the visible patches, and the dispatch weights at the last MoE block (block 11) get reused as per-patch loss coefficients for the distillation and CLS objectives.

![ExPLoRe architecture. The student ViT has six MoE blocks alternating with six vanilla blocks. Each MoE block has a router producing dispatch weights, with the last block's weights serving as per-patch loss coefficients for the distillation and reconstruction objectives.](/blog/per-patch-loss-coupling/architecture.png)

The trick is to make the dispatch weights, not the combine weights, do the loss weighting. For E experts and K loss objectives, you reserve one expert per objective (or learn a mapping) and weight that objective's per-patch loss by the corresponding dispatch weight. The router decides, per patch, what loss recipe matters.

The reason this works is what we call **loss-coupling** in the paper. Because the dispatch weights are differentiable, the loss gradients flow back through the dispatch matrix to the router parameters themselves. The router is not just trained by the usual MoE objective of routing tokens for capacity. It is trained, additionally, by how much its routing decisions affect each loss. Two pressures on the same parameters, and the equilibrium is content-dependent specialization.

## Why dispatch, not combine

This is a small design choice that turned out to be everything. We tried the obvious-looking variant first, which is to use the combine weights as the loss coefficients. Combine weights are normalized per patch so each patch's expert mixture sums to one, which makes them look like a probability distribution and therefore like a natural choice for "what fraction of this patch's loss goes to each expert". They have a fatal degeneracy. Because they are normalized over experts and not over patches, the router can minimize the loss by setting all combine weights to zero for the patches that are hard to learn. The loss vanishes because the weights vanish, not because the model got better. Combine-weight loss weighting collapses to 2.1% k-NN, which is barely above chance. The model basically learns to route nothing to the loss-coupled experts.

Dispatch weights cannot do that because their normalization constraint is per expert, not per patch. The sum of dispatch weights across patches is one for each expert. The router can move weight around between patches but it cannot zero them all out. This forced the per-patch comparison to be in absolute terms, which is what we wanted.

I am proud of finding the degeneracy explicitly, and slightly less proud of taking a few months to realize that was why the combine variant was collapsing. The pivot to dispatch weights came after I sat with the dispatch matrix print-out for an hour and just looked at the rows and columns of the softmax. The asymmetry between the two normalization axes had been staring at me for a while. I had not really looked at it.

## The detach ablation

Once the mechanism worked, the next question was whether it actually was the mechanism or whether the extra parameters were doing all the work. A 2-expert MoE has 116M parameters versus 86M for the no-MoE baseline. 30M extra is not nothing. Maybe what we had built was a way to add 30M parameters with a fancy story attached.

The clean test is to cut just the gradient path. Compute the loss with the dispatch weights as multipliers, but detach them from the computation graph so the loss gradients cannot flow back to the router. Same architecture. Same parameter count. Same compute. Same MoE forward pass. The router is still trained by the usual MoE objective. The only thing we removed is the loss-coupling.

Result: k-NN drops from 75.4% to 73.8%. A 1.6-point gap is small, but the right shape. Without loss-coupling, the MoE still helps a bit through pure capacity, but the bulk of the improvement is gone. The cleaner version of the same point comes from looking at the unweighted MoE, where dispatch weights are computed but not used as loss coefficients at all. That variant lands at 74.1% k-NN, which is *worse* than the 75.7% no-MoE baseline. MoE without loss-coupling is not just neutral compared to a vanilla ViT. It is actively harmful at this scale. The whole mechanism, not just the parameter increment, is what makes it work.

![Block-9 dispatch weights for four images, comparing the loss-coupled model (left two columns: Expert 0 and Expert 1) against the detach variant (right two columns). The coupled model shows spatially coherent, content-dependent routing. The detach model's routing looks closer to noise.](/blog/per-patch-loss-coupling/coupled-vs-detach-heatmaps.png)

The figure above is the visual version of the same finding. Each row is a different image. The left two columns show Expert 0 and Expert 1 dispatch weights from the loss-coupled model. You can see structure: spatially coherent, content-dependent routing. The right two columns are the detach variant. Same backbone, same training schedule, no gradient path. The dispatch weights are noisier, less spatially coherent, and look closer to noise than to signal. The router still routes, because the forward MoE gradient still tells it to. It just routes without learning that some patches matter more to the loss than others.

## An emergent thing I did not predict

One of the supplementary findings I keep coming back to is the CLS specialization. At block 11, which is the block whose dispatch weights we feed back as loss coefficients, Expert 1 concentrates 91% to 95% of its total dispatch weight on the CLS token alone. The CLS token is one out of 197. If routing were uniform, Expert 1 would put about 0.5% of its weight on the CLS token. Instead it puts 95%.

This was not in the design. The model trained against a per-patch token-distillation loss and a global CLS-alignment loss. The router was free to specialize however the gradients wanted. What it did is route the CLS-alignment loss into an expert that effectively becomes a CLS specialist, while the other expert handles the per-patch token distillation across the rest of the image. The two-loss problem was structurally a CLS-vs-patches problem all along, and the router figured that out without us telling it. Even better, this 91-95% concentration emerges *only* at the loss-coupled block. The other MoE blocks, which use the same Soft-MoE machinery but no loss feedback, show no such pattern. That asymmetry between coupled and uncoupled blocks is what convinced me the specialization was driven by loss-coupling and not by something incidental in the architecture.

I find that result satisfying in a way that is hard to write about without sounding sentimental. Pretrained networks doing emergent things has been a tired story for a while, but this one was small and specific. The model found the structure that was implicit in the loss formulation. I have other findings I am prouder of from a paper-acceptance standpoint, but this is the one I most enjoyed staring at.

## The entropy knife-edge

The other unexpected finding is the sharpness of the entropy-regularization transition. Soft-MoE needs entropy regularization to prevent expert collapse, where one expert ends up handling everything and the others stop receiving useful gradient. The regularization weight λ governs how hard you push toward uniform routing. There are two opposing pressures on the dispatch matrix. Entropy pushes toward uniformity, which is the same as no specialization. Loss-coupling pushes toward content-dependent specialization. The whole point of the method is that the equilibrium between those two pressures is useful.

What I did not expect is how knife-edge that equilibrium is. At λ=0.01 the model collapses to 66.9% k-NN, a drop of about 8.5 points. At λ=0.1 it recovers to 75.3%. At λ=0.5 it sits at 75.5%, which is our best 2-expert number. At λ=5.0 it sits at 75.4%, our default. The drop from "stable specialization" to "catastrophic collapse" happens between λ=0.1 and λ=0.01, an order of magnitude. The diagnostics we logged at the collapse make it concrete: the coefficient of variation of dispatch weights jumps 32× across that boundary, and the weighted-to-unweighted loss ratio drops to 0.13. That last number is what convinced me the model was not learning specialization but learning to suppress the loss. It had found a deceptive minimum.

The other surprise is that the optimal λ is expert-count dependent. λ=0.5 is best for 2 experts and degrades 64-expert performance. λ=5.0 is best for 64 and barely affects 2. With more experts, the per-expert dispatch budget gets smaller, so each expert has less room to specialize without crossing the collapse threshold. The regularization has to push harder. The hyperparameter you would tune on the small model is not the one you want on the big model. I wish I had known that from day one.

## What the reviewers caught

Three reviewers, eight major weaknesses, six minor ones. I want to write about one of them, because the rebuttal made the paper better in a way I would not have arrived at on my own.

R1's central pushback was that we report +35% more parameters at the 2-expert scale for only +0.5% k-NN over the no-MoE baseline. Framed on the parameter axis, that is a thin gain. The 64-expert version makes it look even worse because it has 1.86 billion parameters and reaches 80.6% linear probe, which is only +0.1% above CAE v2 at 86 million parameters. If you stare at the parameter column of our comparison table, the paper looks like it bought a 20× parameter increase for tenths of a point.

The thing that R1's pushback forced us to do is plot the same table on the FLOPs axis. Soft-MoE with one slot per expert decouples parameter count from inference cost in a way that vanilla MoE does not. Each MoE layer routes all `N` patch tokens into `E` expert slots, the expert MLPs process those `E` slots rather than `N` inputs, and only the routing projections scale with `E`. The 64-expert configuration has 1.86 billion parameters but only 13.86 GFLOPs of inference cost, which is *less* than the 17.45 GFLOPs of vanilla ViT-Base/16. The 2-expert version is 11.93 GFLOPs, 32% cheaper than every comparator in our table.

![Cost-vs-quality on ViT-Base/16. ExPLoRe E=2 and E=64 sit at the top-left: high linear probe accuracy at substantially lower inference FLOPs than every other published method, all of which sit at vanilla ViT-Base's 17.45 GFLOPs.](/blog/per-patch-loss-coupling/cost-quality.png)

The plot above is what came out of that rebuttal exchange. Every other published method we compare against is bunched at 17.45 GFLOPs because they all use vanilla ViT-Base/16. We sit at the top-left because the Soft-MoE structure with one slot per expert is genuinely a different inference profile, and the parameter count is a misleading proxy for cost on this architecture. The reviewer was right that the parameter framing is not impressive. The compute framing is the one that actually answers the question, and we should have led with it.

I am grateful for that pushback the same way I was grateful for an advisor who once spent thirty minutes interrogating my baseline choice in year two. It is the kind of feedback that does not feel good in the moment and saves you from publishing a worse version of your work later.

## How it scales, and where it does not

![(a) k-NN trajectories across epochs 200-300 for the no-MoE baseline and 2/16/32/64-expert configurations. Stars mark per-configuration peaks. More experts give higher final k-NN, monotonically. (b) Loss weighting effect, isolated. Comparing weighted vs unweighted at matched expert counts: +1.3% at 2 experts, +0.9% at 64 experts. The weighting trick pays for itself at both scales, not just one.](/blog/per-patch-loss-coupling/expert-scaling.png)

Panel (a) shows the scaling curve. Each line is a different expert count, evaluated on k-NN every 10 epochs from 200 to 300. The shape is monotonic: more experts give higher final k-NN, with 64-expert peaking at 76.2%. Panel (b) isolates the loss-coupling contribution at fixed expert count. The weighted-vs-unweighted gap is +1.3% at 2 experts and +0.9% at 64 experts. The mechanism is not just a small-model effect that disappears at scale.

Linear probe scales the same way. 79.6% at 2 experts (up from 76.2% baseline, so +3.4%). 80.6% at 64 experts (+4.4%). I find the linear-probe gap more interesting than the k-NN gap because k-NN is a frozen-feature distance metric, while linear probe is a frozen-feature linear classifier. The +3 to +4 point jump on linear probe means the routing mechanism is reshaping the feature space toward better linear separability, not just preserving the same feature space. That is a structurally different kind of improvement than capacity.

The Efficient Probing protocol ([Psomas et al., ICLR 2026](https://openreview.net/forum?id=BWeBQyCRBd)) is even more direct evidence of this. EP is an attentive probing protocol that recovers patch-level information that CLS-aggregated probes under-measure. Our 2-expert model reaches 80.19% under EP versus 78.77% for the no-MoE baseline, which is +1.41% and about three times the k-NN delta on the same backbones. The pattern R3 hypothesized turned out to be exactly right: the routing improves patch-level representations more than CLS-level, so CLS-aggregated metrics like k-NN under-measure the gain. Fine-grained transfer on Food-101 shows the same pattern at +1.87%.

Where it does not transfer cleanly is dense prediction. Semantic segmentation on ADE20K is the well-known MoE downstream problem. The routing patterns learned during pretraining can fight with the uniform spatial coverage that dense prediction needs, and vanilla MoE finetuning underperforms the non-MoE baseline by 2.5 to 2.9 mIoU. Three adaptation recipes close the gap. Freeze Routing (FR) keeps the pretrained patch-expert assignments fixed during downstream finetuning, following [ST-MoE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.08906). Expert Dropout (ExD) applies dropout to expert outputs to prevent over-reliance on dominant experts, following [Switch Transformer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03961). Freeze Attention (FA) freezes attention weights, also from ST-MoE. The full stack FR+FA+ExD recovers everything: 52.8 mIoU on Token+Pixel (+0.3 over the non-MoE baseline) and 51.1 mIoU on Token+CLS (+0.3 over the non-MoE baseline). The recipes are not ad-hoc, they are documented MoE-transfer techniques, and the fact that the same recipe family closes the gap on both ImageNet finetuning and ADE20K segmentation is what gave reviewers (and me) confidence that the underlying mechanism is real and the transfer protocol is just hard.

## What I am less sure about

A few honest caveats so I do not oversell.

The three-loss extension Token+CLS+Pixel did not give us a measurable gain at our default settings: 75.15% k-NN for the MoE variant versus 75.16% for the matched no-MoE three-loss baseline. Reviewer 1 asked about it directly and the rebuttal answer was "the per-patch routing in our practical config does not yet provide a measurable benefit over symmetric three-loss training". I think the issue is gradient-magnitude conflict between the three losses, which destabilizes specialization at the default entropy weight. The asymmetric per-expert λ that worked for Token+Pixel did not transfer cleanly to dense-mode three-loss training. That is one of the cleanest follow-up questions the paper opens up.

The multi-seed coverage is thinner than I would ideally want. Pretraining at 300 epochs on 4× H100 is genuinely expensive, and re-running 15 configurations at three seeds each is not realistic on our cluster budget. The mechanism-isolation deltas (+1.3% weighted vs unweighted, -1.6% detach) compare configurations sharing pretraining randomness, so seed noise mostly cancels in those internal comparisons. That said, the headline +0.5% k-NN over the no-MoE baseline does live within the noise floor that you would expect at this scale. The compute-axis reframing matters more than the small k-NN number suggests.

The 64-expert configuration is something I am genuinely conflicted about. It works, and the cost-quality story holds because of how Soft-MoE structures inference. But 1.86 billion parameters trained from scratch in 300 epochs on ImageNet is not a configuration I would defend as practical for most use cases. The 2-expert version is the recommendation. The 64-expert version is the existence proof that the mechanism scales, and I would not want anyone to use it as a production model.

## What I would do differently

The thing I keep coming back to, more than any individual finding, is that I should have built the mechanism-isolation comparisons in week 3, not month 8. The detach ablation, the unweighted-MoE baseline, the dispatch-vs-combine comparison. Every one of those is a small experiment that costs a fraction of a pretraining run. If I had built them at the start, I would have known by month 2 whether the dispatch-weight reuse was real or whether I was looking at parameter inflation. I would have stopped tweaking entropy weights for weeks at the wrong λ region. I would have caught the combine-weight degeneracy in a 2-day sanity check instead of a multi-month detour.

When you build the right diagnostic experiment, you stop guessing. When you do not, you spend a year refreshing curves.

The other thing I would do differently is plot on the FLOPs axis from day one. Looking at the cost-quality figure now, the story is obviously a per-FLOP story, and the parameter-axis framing makes ExPLoRe look weaker than it is on the actual axis it competes on. Reviewers caught that in week one of the rebuttal. I had been staring at the wrong axis for the whole project.

## What is next

I defended in April. I joined an Amazon Robotics team in Berlin a few weeks ago. ExPLoRe is the last paper I will publish from the lab. The next year of work is mostly going to be language models for robotics, which is a different game from masked image modeling on ImageNet.

If you are working on multi-objective pretraining and want to try the dispatch-weight trick, the [repo](https://github.com/aicip/ExPLoRe) has a smoke-test config that runs in about 15 minutes on a single GPU. The paper is on arXiv at [2606.31201](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.31201) and the code is at [aicip/ExPLoRe](https://github.com/aicip/ExPLoRe). Issues are open, and if anything in the paper or code reads wrong, the issue tracker is faster than email.

I am also genuinely curious how the dispatch-weight idea generalizes outside MIM. Any multi-objective setup where the objectives have spatial or token-level structure is a candidate. Multi-task language modeling is the obvious one. Multi-modal pretraining is another. I have not tried any of that. If you do, I would love to hear about it in a comment below or on the repo.
